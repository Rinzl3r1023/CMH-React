## **What Does "Throwing Stones" Mean in Marketing?**

Throwing stones means addressing the bad experiences your audience has had with other solutions in your category. But you do it without directly attacking a named competitor.

Airbnb does this brilliantly. Their ads say things like "somewhere in a hotel..." and then show the problem. They never name Hilton or Hampton Inn. But the viewer's brain fills that in immediately. Why? Because the viewer has been there. They've lived that experience. So when Airbnb surfaces that frustration, the connection is instant.

There's a right way and a wrong way to do this. The right way is to be specific about a real, shared experience your audience has had. The wrong way is to be abrasive or to attack people rather than problems. You're not trying to make someone feel stupid for their past choices. You're trying to show them a better path.

A chiropractor, for example, deals with people who've heard others call the field "quackery." The right stone to throw isn't "other practitioners are bad." It's "here's why that quick-adjustment approach doesn't work for chronic issues — and here's what we do differently." That's respectful. And it opens doors.

## **What Can Airbnb's Ads Teach You About Marketing Differentiation?**

Airbnb ran a series of ads that are worth studying closely. Each one targets a completely different audience. And each one uses the same framework: show the undesired state, then transform it.

### **The Family Travel Ad: Selling Space and Privacy**

The first ad — I'll call it the family travel one — opens in a hotel room. The room stays the same size year after year. But the kids keep growing. They get taller, wider, harder to contain. The room starts to feel absurd. A dog appears. The cramped space becomes almost comical.

Then the transition happens. The family moves into an Airbnb. Suddenly, the kids have their own area. The parents are on a separate floor. They have privacy. Everyone has room to breathe.

Notice what Airbnb is not selling. They're not selling square footage. They're selling the feeling of a family vacation that actually works — where the parents can decompress while the kids are still being kids. The transformation from frustrated to free is the product.

None of that was an accident. The dog, the cramped doorways, the same frozen room while the family grows — every visual detail serves the point. This is marketing that works beneath the surface before you even realize what's happening.

### **The Girls Trip Ad: Solving the Bathroom Problem**

The second ad focuses on a group of women sharing a hotel room on a trip. The problem? One bathroom. One mirror. One tiny counter. Everyone's fighting for space to get ready.

The music even shifts as the frustration builds — it goes from light and fun to strained and tense. Then Airbnb flips it. Each woman gets her own space to get ready. The mood lifts. They head out together, looking and feeling great.

This ad speaks to a very specific avatar: women traveling with friends who want to feel their best during the trip. Airbnb didn't try to speak to everyone. They spoke to that one person.

One thing worth noting: the music choice later in that ad skews toward a younger demographic. A 40-something audience might see it and feel it wasn't for them. That's the risk of avatar confusion. When you try to appeal to a broader group, you may end up speaking clearly to no one. Know your person. Stick to your message.

### **The Bedtime Ad: Romance and Reconnection**

The third ad tackles a different pain: the loss of privacy when you travel with a young child. In the hotel version, the child ends up between the parents. The lights stay on. The romance disappears. No one goes to bed when they want to.

Then Airbnb shows the transformation. The couple is in Paris. The child is asleep in a separate room. The parents are sharing a glass of wine together, looking out over the city. The fireworks go off. The entire mood shifts.

Airbnb is not selling a bigger hotel room. They're selling rekindled romance on a family vacation. They identified an emotional outcome — the privacy and intimacy couples lose when they travel with young children — and made that the core of the ad. The features of the property just prove that the transformation is possible.

## **Why Do Features Fail to Sell on Their Own?**

Because people don't buy features. They buy outcomes and feelings.

We all buy with emotion first. Then we validate with logic. If an ad leads with "two bedrooms, full kitchen, private entrance," you're asking someone to do the emotional work themselves. Most won't bother. But if you show them what their life looks like after they say yes — the romantic evening, the relaxed parents, the girls trip that actually lets everyone get ready in peace — then the features become the proof. They answer the question: "How does that transformation happen?"

Airbnb nails this. They show you the feeling first. Then the features support it naturally. Think of it like spaghetti and meatballs. The transformation is the meatball. The features are the spaghetti. They belong together. But you lead with the meatball.

So the question for your business is this: what is your audience's real desired outcome? Not the service you provide. The feeling they want on the other side of it.

## **What Is Avatar Splintering and Why Does It Matter?**

Avatar splintering means creating different messages for different segments of your audience, instead of one message for everyone.

Airbnb didn't run one ad. They ran a series — families, friend groups, couples — each targeting a specific avatar. Each message was sharp because it spoke to one person's exact situation. If they had blended all three into a single ad, none of the messages would have landed.

When you're starting out, focus on one avatar. Master that message. As you grow, you can add more avatar profiles and develop messaging for each. But trying to speak to five different types of people with one piece of content usually means you're speaking clearly to none of them.

Give each avatar a name if it helps. Think of the Robertsons — a family with teenage kids who care about space, privacy, and bringing the dog. That's a real person with real concerns. Speak directly to them. The ad practically writes itself.

## **Did Airbnb Take Market Share Away From Hotels?**

Actually, no. And this is one of the most important lessons here.

I interviewed Chip Conley on my podcast some time ago. Chip served as Airbnb's head of global hospitality and was a key mentor to CEO Brian Chesky. Chip told me something that genuinely surprised me. Hotels, he said, didn't see a significant drop in revenue when Airbnb took off. Why? Because Airbnb reached people who hadn't been traveling at all, or who previously stayed with family and friends. They didn't take a piece of the existing market. They created a new one.

That's a blue ocean. Airbnb found people who were underserved — people who couldn't afford or didn't want the hotel experience — and built something specifically for them.

You can do the same thing. And you don't have to win a war against bigger competitors. You just have to matter deeply to the people you matter to. Find the gap. Build the offer. Speak directly to the person who has been overlooked.

## **Key Takeaways**

- Throw stones at the category, not a specific competitor. Let your audience make the connection from their own experience.
- Sell the transformation first. Features prove the outcome; they don't replace it.
- Use avatar splintering. One specific message for one specific person is far more powerful than a broad message for everyone.
- Non-abrasive positioning works. "Some trips are better in an Airbnb" earns trust because it's honest. Overclaiming repels people.
- You don't need to compete directly. Airbnb created new demand rather than stealing from hotels. Find your underserved audience and build for them.
- Repositioning alone — before any significant ad spend — can dramatically change revenue and client quality.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### ***What does "throwing stones" mean in marketing?***

Throwing stones means addressing the bad experiences your audience has had with other solutions in your category — without naming or directly attacking a specific competitor. The goal is to surface a frustration your audience already feels, so they connect the dots themselves. Done right, it builds strong affinity and opens people to considering your offer.

### ***What is avatar splintering?***

Avatar splintering means creating distinct messages for distinct segments of your audience rather than one message for everyone. Each ad or piece of content speaks to one specific type of person — their situation, their frustration, their desired outcome. When you try to speak to too many avatars at once, every message gets weaker. Start with one. Add more as you grow.

### ***Why should you sell transformation instead of features in marketing?***

Because people buy outcomes and feelings, not features. We buy with emotion first and validate with logic second. A feature list asks the audience to imagine the benefit themselves — most won't. But when you show the transformation (before → after), the audience connects emotionally and your features become the logical proof that the transformation is possible.

### ***How did Airbnb differentiate against hotel chains?***

Airbnb didn't compete directly with hotels. Instead, they reached an underserved audience — people who weren't traveling at all, or who stayed with family and friends because hotels felt out of reach. By building for that group, they created new demand rather than stealing existing market share. Their ads reinforced this by selling family privacy, friend-group space, and couple reconnection — outcomes hotels rarely address.

### ***Can repositioning an offer increase revenue without running ads?***

Yes. Repositioning alone can have a significant impact. I worked with one client in the health and wellness space who repositioned his offer without running significant ads. That move grew his revenue from roughly $800,000 to around $1.2 million. The key was understanding exactly how his audience was underserved and building messaging around that gap.
