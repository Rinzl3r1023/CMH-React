Most people use AI like a vending machine. Insert a question, grab the answer, walk away. It's the smallest possible way to use the most powerful tool of our lifetime.

The people actually pulling ahead with AI aren't the ones with the cleverest prompts. They're the ones who stopped treating it like a search box and started treating it like a partner they think with. That one shift — from transactional to relational — is the whole game. Here's how I actually do it, and why it changed the way I run my business.

## First, about the fear

You've heard the loud version: AI is going to replace us all. Here's my honest take. Yes — some jobs go away. That's true of every major technology. The tractor did it. The cell phone did it. Technology has always eliminated the need for certain roles — and it has always opened a new opportunity next to it, where the work becomes [reposition and reskill](/great-weeding-out/).

So I'm not a trend-chaser, and I'm not an early adopter. I'm a timely adapter — and now is the time to adapt to and with AI. But to what degree you need to is a real question, and that's my job: to help you get out of overwhelm and figure out what's actually applicable to you, and what isn't. I watch the firehose so you don't have to. You get the curated version of what's meaningful for the business you actually run.

## Why most people use AI like a vending machine

I think the vending-machine habit comes from a simple thing: AI looks like a Google search box. So we treat it like one. We type a short query — "best chiropractor Austin" — the way we've been trained to for twenty years, and we grab the list it spits back.

To understand why that's leaving 90% of the value on the table, you have to understand what Google actually solved — and what it didn't.

## Discovery vs. curation (the thing Google never solved)

Before Google, you did two jobs yourself: discovery and curation. You wanted to research something, you went to a library. You scoured the aisles for the right section. You pulled the books, then you curated what mattered inside those books. Anyone who wrote research papers knows how arduous that was.

Google removed the discovery part. That's it. It didn't solve curation — it handed you a directory of links and left the curating to you. That's why a lazy prompt like "best restaurants near me" worked: you were still going to do the curation yourself by clicking around.

Large language models — ChatGPT, Claude, Grok — do both. Discovery and curation. This was always the next evolution of what Google started, and honestly, the fact that Google didn't arrive at a Gemini-type solution a decade sooner is a Blockbuster-level miss. They solved discovery, dominated the market, and then spent years on Google Glass and Google Plus instead of building on their one core value proposition. Curation was the obvious next thing to solve. They left it on the table.

The practical takeaway for you: because AI does both, it needs your help with scope. The moment you stop treating it as "insert token, get answer" and start giving it context, you're already ahead of the pack.

## "Thought partner" isn't a metaphor

Here's the shift: don't treat it transactionally. Treat it relationally.

I don't mean become best friends with your AI. I mean lean in with curiosity. Ask questions. Give it context. Open up voice-to-text and tell it what you're thinking about your situation — not just what you need it to do. I never opened a blank window and typed "do XYZ." I gave it context first. When that context is meaningful and evergreen, I save it to memory. When it's short-term — like when I'm building something — I save it to a project file.

In other words, I treat it exactly how I named it: a partner. And I make sure it has what it needs to do its job.

## What I actually load into mine

This is where it gets real. My thought partner knows my business's master documents, our plan for the year, my team members and their strengths and weaknesses. It knows me — my personality assessments, my strengths and weaknesses, even my genetic profile, my health history, my supplement and operating protocols. My tools are connected, so it can reach into any of those buckets and pull out what's relevant the moment I need it.

And most of it happens on the go. I'm giving you this very piece from my phone, on a walk. The other day I saw an email come in, asked my thought partner to research the person and draft a response, and sent it from the sidewalk before dinner with my wife and a couple of friends. Think about what that used to take — the time to sit down and craft something both thoughtful and timely. I did both, standing on a corner.

## I don't just use it — I build with it

For me it's not a glorified assistant. In a lot of cases, I use it as a CTO. I've built custom apps with it. I've revamped an entire website in an afternoon. I've built a tool that packages our methodology so our clients can execute on our knowledge, not just consume it. That last one matters: the opportunity isn't just doing your own work faster — it's turning what you know into something other people can run.

## The part nobody talks about: it covers your blind spots

I think often about Tony Stark — and his relationship with both JARVIS and Pepper Potts. In a lot of ways, both of them were covering his blind spots as a leader. And here's the thing: he had both. AI didn't replace the humans in his life. It was its own category — a unicorn that didn't exist before. So don't hear "thought partner" as a contradiction of ["AI won't replace people."](/where-ai-ends-and-you-begin/) Both matter. Both have their place.

Here's the personal version. I'm a very ADD person. My thoughts go everywhere; I can get way off track. So I built that into my thought partner. I told it: this is who I am. I will lose sight of the signal. If you see it happening, hold me accountable. Ask me for clarification. Be more thorough here, less thorough there. If I need encouragement, encourage me. I even trained it on what I call "middle gear" — so when I get activated about something, it helps me get into the right posture, get the raw reaction out of my system, and actually process the situation instead of reacting to it.

You cannot do that with a human hire. When you bring someone on, you take the good with the bad — you can request they change how they operate with you, but their personality is their personality. With a thought partner, you can build the exact partner you need. That's not a small thing. That's a category that didn't exist a few years ago.

## The opportunity that's actually in front of us

Solopreneurs make up the majority of businesses in this country, and a meaningful slice of the whole economy — and most of them are doing it alone. So here's the question that gets me out of bed: what happens when AI thought partners expand what one person can do? What if that solo operator becomes productive enough to grow — enough to finally hire humans back into human roles? That's the opportunity and the challenge in front of us.

And I don't say any of this as a rah-rah mantra. I'm saying it as someone who had a genuine existential crisis over this — months of nights up until 3 or 4 a.m. reading about AI, listening to people predict the replacement of everything. I came out the other side through immersion and application, not theory and speculation. I built my own understanding until I was confident enough to speak to it — and then I built most of my brand around it.

## My challenge to you

Start getting excited about the possibility instead of scared of the threat. Seek the opportunity and you'll go far. Identify the gaps and cover them. Find the things that are blind to you and see if they can be revealed.

But all of it starts with one move: drop the magic-genie, give-me-an-answer, Google mode — and start pushing the tool as far as you can, with curiosity and a little bit of play. Do that, and I think you'll realize you're doing something that feels like world-building in a digital reality.

## The takeaways

- Stop using AI transactionally; use it relationally. The value isn't the answer it gives you — it's the better thinking it pulls out of you.
- Google solved discovery. AI solves discovery and curation. That's why it needs your context and scope, not just a one-line query.
- Give it context before commands. Who you are, how you work, your business, your goals. Save the evergreen stuff to memory, the short-term stuff to project files.
- It covers blind spots a human hire can't — because you can build the exact partner you need.
- The winners won't have the best prompts. They'll bring [the best judgment](/ai-pilot-not-prompt-engineer/) — and be willing to think out loud.
