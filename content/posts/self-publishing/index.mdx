## **Oh, How the Writing Game has Changed**

One of the cool things about writing an installment on a topic that I can't fall back on experience is that I'm literally regurgitating information that I'm learning along the way.

I've been scouring the globe for the best advice I can find and working my network to get as many opinions as possible.

From 4X New York Times Best Selling Author, Tucker Max, to Todd Herman and even folks that specialize in helping people get book deals like my guest on the pod today, Lindsay Preston.

One thing has been resounding...

The writing game is a changing and changing fast!

Back in the day, you pretty much had to go through a Traditional Publisher if you wanted your book to get noticed and make sense for the amount of time you were going to sink into it.

But no more!

Entrepreneurs and people of all walks of life are now able to turn and publish a book in as little as 60 days!

What's more is they are going to see a far heftier amount of those royalties in return.

So, why would you NOT just go ahead and self-publish or go the indie route?

We're going to explore the in's and out's here today so you can make the best decision possible for your penmanship journey.

## **Traditional Publishers Are a Bit of a Monopoly**

The system is rigged, as Tucker Max put it to me.

The New York based Publishing Houses do what they do, make a lot of money doing it and largely have the in-roads you'll need to get the street cred you may be searching for.

In fact, you could literally outsell 99% of the folks on the New York Times Best Seller list and STILL not crack the list yourself.

Why?

Because it comes down to reporting and what they "count" towards your actual sales figure.

In other words, you could sell a bazillion copies of your book on Amazon but it won't go towards your NYT's efforts.

These big time Publishing Houses have networks and Sales Reps that ensure your book is on the book shelves at local bookstores around the country.

Those relationships and that level of support is going to be mighty hard to compete with as a first-time, indie or self-published writer.

Now, you may have other folks like The Wall Street Journal and USA Today that will recognize a self-published author but apparently it's super spotty and inconsistent.

That said, if your goal is to get the fancy New York Times Best Seller label on your LinkedIn profile or plopped right next to your name at every intro you ever receive, going with a Traditional Publisher seems to make all the sense in the world.

## **Show Me the Money!**

If you're using a book as Lead Gen or a Free + Shipping type of deal, I personally wouldn't go the Traditional Publisher route.

In fact, Publishers usually only pay out about 7-25% royalties with ~10% being the average on all sales of your book, whereas Amazon is a hearty 70%!

I did the math just for the sake of comparison.

To keep it simple, I did 100,000 copies.

Which, by the way, is not a ridiculous figure!

I also assumed your book retailed at $15, which is just completely arbitrary and makes no impact on the point I'm making here.

If you received what seems to be the standard 10% from a Publishing House, you're looking at $150,000 in your pocket.

By contrast, Self-Publishing AND still selling 100,000 copies nets you a whopping $1.05MM!

Now, I get you may not be as savvy at getting your book in the hands of readers so let's say you only did 15,000 copies compared to 100,000 with their help.

You STILL make more money coming in at $157,000!

That's just crazy talk in my book (no pun intended).

## **You Lose Your Soul...And Your Creative Freedom**

The biggest thing I'm reading in terms of cons with going the Traditional Publisher route is Authors feel like they lose their creative liberties.

Once you sign a book deal, they essentially own the story.

They can change your cover, the title and even elements of your content!

What's more is most of these agreements can be three book deals with non-compete clauses.

Meaning, if you write another book under your name and within the same confines of that genre, you're probably going to find yourself in some nice legal trouble.

That's a tall order if you ask me.

Knowing I'm skimping on the return AND I've got to potentially do it THREE times or just sit and wait out my non-compete is something I would really think twice about.

I also read where your royalties agreement extends 70 years beyond the death of the writer.

From most of the writers I researched, this seemed to be the prevailing complaint.

Not the money but the liberty to write what they wanted and how they wanted to convey their own message.

## **Better Plan on the Long Haul**

Originally, I figured you just reached out to Penguin Random House or another big time publisher, developed a contact and pitched your idea for a book.

Silly me...

You're going to need an agent.

The agent is going to want to see a semi-finished product of what you intend to write and/or a written proposal.

From my research, this process can take up to a full calendar year.

The agent will then go out on your behalf and pitch the book to Publishers.

They may want you to change X, Y, or Z in order for them to be interested or maybe even change the genre.

Todd Herman told me he was asked to write in the Business Genre but felt his book really didn't fit in the bucket.

This process, plus all of the editing, finalizing agreements and getting your book out on store shelves can take another 2-3 years!

In other words, it's an incredibly slow process in comparison to self-publishing or indie writing.

If you're going to go that route, I hope you're not counting on the money anytime soon.

Apparently, you can get an Advance in capital, let's say of $10,000, but that money is going to come out of your royalties.

Meaning, your royalty checks wouldn't kick in until you sold $10,000 worth of royalty amount in your favor.

## **Legal Jargon and Protecting Yourself**

One of the other interesting pieces I was able to glean from my research was how some people get taken advantage of at the negotiating table.

This can extend to your arrangement with global sales, audio versions of the book and the aforementioned non-compete.

I'm not expert in this area, especially given I've not gone through the process myself, but it sounds like this an area to really consider and be mindful of.

## **Conclusion on Traditional Publisher**

If you want to be known as a writer and have committed your career to that idea, go this route.

At least that's what I would do.

If that's me, I'm playing the long-game and I also recognize legacy is far more important to me than the money.

That, or I want to be remembered and known as a thought-leader in my space.

If I have some grand and novel way of thinking or approaching a problem that I think is revolutionary enough to change the ways in which people live their lives, conduct their business or both...absolutely.

It's a prestige play like no other.

Your credibility is going to be so much higher than just some guy that sells books on Amazon.

It's going to put you in BIG rooms with BIG crowds to share your BIG idea.

And ultimately, it's probably going to evolve into something grandiose for you as a result.

You may be able to build an entirely new business around this idea like Simon Sinek did in "Start with Why."

Or maybe it's a humanitarian play and you want it in front of as many eyeballs as possible.

It's not implausible you could do that on your own but Publishing Houses are certainly a far more well-oiled machine at what they do than the novice self-publisher, to say the least.

The other reason I would say to go with a Publishing House is the structure.

The editing teams, the creative help, meeting deadlines, structure, input, etc.

Publishing Houses are going to refine your story way better than you probably can.

Their job is to make money and thus, are going to invest in the asset that is your book.

I recognize some people need that structure.

Not to say you can't get it otherwise, but it's certainly proven.

## **The Self-Publishing and Indie Movement!**

Never in the history of man has it been easier to publish your very own book and become an author.

Isn't that amazing!?

Many business folks and entrepreneurs alike are leveraging the power of that exposure and credibility to really leverage their brands.

It's often an intro to their BIG idea or services and then you're presented with another level on the value ladder to continue your learning.

I've not seen anyone do this better than Russell Brunson.

I finished reading his books and was literally reaching for my computer to learn more about Click Funnels.

It's an amazing intro to his concepts and he has the perfect solution in place to back it up.

If you aim to do something similar, that's your model to use, in my opinion.

The other benefit to going the self or indie publisher route is you don't necessarily have the pressure of velocity of sales.

In the Publisher route, if you want that New York Times Best Seller, you better have at least 5,000 copies pre-ordered and probably at least 5 to 10k sold in the first week alone!

And that's just to make the list.

However, you don't have that sort of pressure with self or indie publishing.

You can do what's called an Evergreen format.

Which means it's just an ongoing and automated sales process over a long period of time or no defined period of time.

With a launch, you are really hustling for that one specific date and if you swing and miss...ooops!

Now, that's not to say you can't continue to sell the book regardless but keep in mind one of the main reasons you WOULD go the Publisher route is for the acclaim and not necessarily the money.

Evergreen funnels are amazing because again, they are wonderful front-end offers to a cold audience.

This introduces them to who you are and what you have to offer.

It's likely you can build super-fans in this manner and still get the street cred of being a published author, just not AS much as your author that went with a Traditional Publishing House.

I've now been told on many occasions writing and having a book is the new business card in this day and age.

Many speaking gigs won't even consider you unless you are a published author.

So, if that's your goal and are bursting at the seems to get this done, time wise, you're looking at a much shorter period before that's realistic.

## **Introducing the Hybrid Model**

Now, the hybrid model is certainly not new by any stretch.

But's it's probably new to you, as it likely should be.

The problem with explaining the hybrid model to the degree I understand it is that it seems to be very malleable.

Meaning, there's not really a black and white answer.

This modality seems to just operate in the shades of gray contingent upon what the author needs and/or is looking for.

Agents have shifted and started their own publishing companies rather than just serve as a middle man.

Some small publishers are partnered with larger publishers and thus serve as a facilitator of sorts.

Usually, in a hybrid model arrangement, the author is going to take over a sizable potion of the financial risk but stands to have far higher royalties in addition to more creative freedom.

Technically, we've talked about "indie" writing and it falls inside of the Hybrid jurisdiction.

If this is something that interests you or if you feel you need more of an a-la-carte approach...meaning, you want the benefits and assistance of a Publisher but want to earn more of the money on the back-end AND maintain creative liberties, I would challenge you to explore this avenue further before making a decision.

Truth be told, it's so complex and has so many variations I really don't think it serves us well to get too far off on a tangent here as it's probably better served as an installment on it's own.

## **So, What Should You Do!?**

How about this...

Let me tell you what I plan to do and why.

I've found that observing someone's thought process usually helps me navigate my own terrain.

I recently was in Seattle to have Keith Krance and his team work with us directly on our business models as it relates to funnel building and Facebook Ads.

What amazed me most was just watching them go through the mechanics of their processes.

That sort of insight is something I could have never gleaned from just buying one of their programs online.

And so hopefully I can replicate that here with this discussion.

I want to write books, always have.

If you've read this far, I assume you like my writing style (or you're just suffering through this post).

I'm able to communicate a message through writing that, I think, vastly exceeds other forms of communication.

That said, I love doing my podcast and you would probably never guess what I just said otherwise.

But writing just ignites something inside of me.

It's like my heart opens up and just spills out onto the keyboard.

You may not gather that from this practical application post but check any of my other installments where I talk about my story and you're like to see what I mean.

It is important to me to become a New York Times Best Selling Author.

I want to make an impact.

I don't necessarily care about the money...at least not totally.

But to change a million lives?

Man, that's the dream.

And so I'm sitting here with this dilemma.

I see the numbers I gave to you, it makes no sense to jump out of the gates and NOT self-publish.

And so what I've done is separated my content into two categories.

One set that I feel is practical advice and will get your business up and running in no time!

The other bucket is my big idea that probably doesn't tie into a business-related product, although I wouldn't say it's precluded as being such.

My practical advice content is very much suited to set up an upsell.

It's the perfect application to my value ladder and thus, I'll probably just go ahead and self-publish.

I can get it done faster and really ramp up my efforts.

It's only going to add value (or perceived value) to everything else I'm selling.

Plus, I get the street cred in terms of setting myself apart from just another guy selling stuff on the internet.

Realistically, my plan is to get this done in Quarter 1 of 2019.

Now, for my long-term, BIG idea...

The one I think could impact lives and change the entire way we approach our day(s), that deserves to be in the Traditional Publishing bucket.

I love what that idea stands for.

It's one of those transformative ideas that I think deserves to be laced up next to a big time Publishing House.

The good thing about this strategy, at least for me, is by self-publishing the first one (or two), is you get way more negotiating power when you do reach out to a Publishing House through your Agent.

Further, it's entirely possible they discover YOU through your work if it's really solid and performs well.

Win-WIN!

Amirite?

What's more is you never know what can happen.

The Martian was self-published and ended up being picked up by a Publishing House.

That then turned into a movie deal that starred Matt Damon and was a Box Office HIT!

What's ironic and funny about this approach is it's the exact same way I would approach a startup business.

I like proving something first.

Whether it's me, my team or the idea/concept itself, you stand a much better chance at a solid investment deal if you're first able to show something tangible in terms of results.

But, some ideas are so grand it does need the backing of an investor to even get off the ground.

Starting to see the parallels here?

So look, whatever you decide to do I hope you continue to do your research.

We have authors on our podcast, Entrepreneur Hour, all the time and I would invite you to follow us and hear their stories.

And when I say authors, I'm talking the New York Times variety that can maybe start to provide an example to you of what ideas are worth going through a Publishing House.

I'd be curious to see and bet you'll listen to what they have to say in a whole new light!

Happy writing!
