#### **Recording:**


So, this is going to be contingent upon what type of device you have (Mac or PC) and whether you intend to do interviews or solo.

We'll address both...

For interviews, I highly recommend [Zencastr](http://zencastr.com).

It's an unbelievable platform and I'm going to tell you why...

But before I do that, let me just tell you to avoid Skype like the plague.

We reached a point of having issues on about 50% of our recordings using Skype.

Now, you're going to get guests that push back on not using Skype because it's super mainstream and easy to use.

Fortunately for you, people like myself are fighting that battle and getting others acclimated to the change to Zencastr.

Zencastr is an inherently different tool than Skype.

It's a cloud-based recording studio...meaning it was made specifically for podcasting.

It records audio on BOTH ends and then uploads those files to the cloud when recording has been completed.

That matters because audio quality goes to hell when you record both files on your end.

It doesn't matter if your guest is using the most expensive and professional audio equipment, it's still going to sound bad.

By recording locally first, Zencastr takes that native file and gives you access to it.

In that situation, your guest will sound just as good, if not better, than you.

In fact, some of my episodes you literally can't tell that they were done remote.

When doing solo recordings, I also use my Zencastr and record myself but you can also use your editing tool (we'll get to that).

Honestly, anything you have to record computer audio on your end will suffice, I wouldn't overthink it.

For a long time, I just used [Screencast-O-Matic](https://screencast-o-matic.com/) and stripped the audio from those files in editing.

It was a tool I was already very familiar with and proved to be a seamless transition.

You'll want to test your audio before every single recording and make sure your system settings didn't somehow get thrown out of wack.

If you're audio levels are set too high or too low, you're going to get a very poor product.

Most mics and Zencastr do a pretty good job of leveling out the settings but it's something to always note.



#### **Editing:**


Editing is going to be your absolute least favorite part of podcasting.

I've literally never met a podcaster that didn't say that.

It sucks, plain and simple.

But when you get good at it, you can get that time down substantially or just outsource it.

At this point, I have an editor in the Philippines that just does magic.

There are services you can hire but they're usually fairly expensive.

I recommend you do it yourself at first and then outsource.

You need to know what's involved so you can at least speak their language.

I've seen people get ripped off by just throwing money at the problem.

Now, I know many people just use default programs on their computer and I'm a PC guy, so let me tell you what I use.

I'm a big fan of Sony Vegas' Movie Studio Platinum...

I'll show you why in this image.



You see how the audio wavelengths are super visible?

Not all editors have that feature as prevalent as Movie Studio.

When you're editing, this is everything.

Seriously...

Because interviews on Zencastr are going to download as two separate files, which you want.

It's so much easier to fix problems if they arise and it gives you way more control.

Sometimes, I cut stuff out that I said if I interrupted the guest or if I felt it was disrupting the conversation.

Plus, the quality of the audio is way better by recording on separate tracks, as we discussed.

But you may experience what's called audio drift...aka Freddy Krueger.

Audio Drift robbed me of sleep for many moons.

Basically, it's when one of the files drags out a bit longer than the other, even though the conversation started and ended at the same time.

It can be a result of a variety of issues like outdated browser, internet connectivity issues or too many tabs open or programs running.

Either way, it totally sucks and can motivate you to never do a podcast recording ever again.

When editing, I'm just making sure my files line up properly and then I publish my Version 1.0 of a guest interview.

I take Version 1.0 and open up a new window...this is primarily for simplicity reasons. It's just a preference.

We use custom bumpers on the front and tail end of our interviews in addition to an intro and outro file.

The intro music was a purchased music file from [Audio Jungle](https://audiojungle.net/) and a hired voice over actor on [Fiverr](https://www.fiverr.com/).

The outro for me is two different music files that I merged together to create the effect I wanted and then overlayed with my bumper.

The bumper at the end of my show is just a reminder to subscribe and leave a review in addition to my major takeaways.

As you'll see here, it can get a bit discombobulated looking and that's why I prefer to just edit and insert the interview file rather than try to do all of this together.



I go to make a movie, do it in an MP3 format and highest audio quality...DONE!

#### **Advanced Tips:**


Alright, so we've covered the literal bare minimum to get a show up and running.

- I wanted to provide just a few more tips to get you off on your way to success. Prepare questions for your guests before interviews and send them, plus your Zencastr link, at least 24 hours in advance. This makes you look legit and like you've actually done your homework. I try to make sure I ask them questions they haven't been asked before, specifically if it's someone that gets interviewed quite often. I share my questions via a Google Doc so we can collaborate. PLEASE do not have an "interview flow" or show up with no questions prepared. This is literally my biggest pet peeve. Show that you actually took interest in who they are and what type of value they can provide to your audience. Seriously, I see this as highly offensive when those things aren't prepared for me ahead of time.



- Re-purpose your content on social media as a blog post. I use Canva (with high-res images) to design my blog feature image and social media post images but it needs to be eye-catching and clear as to what it involves. I know this because I had been posting for two years only to learn that most people had no idea they were podcast episodes...arrrrghh. I would use a content scheduling tool like Buffer of Co-Schedule.



- Hire a Virtual Assistant from OnlineJobs.ph to help with editing and content scheduling. This will drastically reduce the amount of time spent on post-production requirements. I also recommend creating canned email responses and having your VA email the guest with links, when published.



- Install Streak for Gmail to track email views. Seriously, if you want to land big guests, this is a must. You need to know if these people are opening your emails or not and if you don't know that you have no idea what you should say in a follow up email.



- Use a scheduling tool like Calendly to have guests schedule their episode. It's quick, easy and very intuitive when it comes to sinking up with you calendar. You can also put in instructions with links when they confirm. Plus, they'll be reminded 24 hours in advance of the interview about the upcoming event. I've had ZERO no-shows in the history of my podcast because we've established effective lines of automated communication.



- When you reach out to a big guest, or any guest, you want to create a canned response that is concise but also gives them enough details as to why they should come on your show. You need to present this as a benefit for them and not just you wanting free content. You can include things like the monthly downloads you receive, notable guests you have, who you serve or just who you are and why you're uniquely qualified to have them on your show. I ALWAYS have these emails come from my Virtual Assistant and NEVER from me. It makes you look legit and not like a hobbyist. This is a little part of my secret formula as to how I've consistently landed some of the biggest names on the planet in entrepreneurship.



- Get reviews and subscribers at any and all costs. This is going to really boost your ranking on iTunes and tell them it's a show worth listening to. I don't want you to get caught up in the semantics of iTunes rankings but it's good to keep moving that needle forward, whenever possible.



- If echo is a problem in your recording room, you can record in a walk-in closet (seriously, not joking) or buy [acoustic panels](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B010GPFRUQ/ref=as_li_qf_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=chrismharris1-20&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=B010GPFRUQ&linkId=b97fb28e1eb9ed1d76d20fd21ee0631a) for your walls (must apply liberally for results). I've also heard thick curtains can help reduce reverb but sometimes the room is just not great for recording. If it's really bad and none of these things work or you don't feel like camping out in your closet, you can whip out the credit card for one of these [sound recording booth boxes](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B0117DB5TI/ref=as_li_qf_asin_il_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=chrismharris1-20&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=B0117DB5TI&linkId=64632aacc8488a22b636aa936b25b590). This is the most affordable one I could find that seemingly had great reviews. They have some that are wayyy more expensive if you choose to go that route and want something more "official," so to speak. I don't use these because my microphone, as mentioned, is simply amazing.
